## 1. What “directional trading” really means (and why it’s politically sensitive)

### Naive definition

Directional trading =

> “I think price will go up/down, so I buy/sell.”

That _is_ part of it — but it’s the **least sophisticated** form.

### Why firms hide it

- Shareholders of **merchant energy companies** don’t want hedge funds in disguise
    
- Utilities and oil majors are expected to be _stable_, not speculative
    
- Losses are punished harder than gains are rewarded
    

So directional trading often appears as:

- “hedging”
    
- “customer-related activity”
    
- “balance optimisation”
    

Sometimes **even senior management doesn’t want to know** the full truth.

> The hedge/speculation boundary is deliberately fuzzy — because enforcing a clean line would kill profitability.

## 2. “Directional trading is zero-sum” — true but misleading

At first glance:

- If I make money, someone else must lose it
    
- Hedge fund A vs hedge fund B → one dies, one wins
    

Kaminski acknowledges this reality — especially in **crowded trades**.

### The duellist analogy

Two funds:

- opposite positions
    
- high leverage
    
- similar information
    

Outcome:

- one blows up
    
- the other looks like a genius
    

This _does_ happen, frequently.

---

## 3. The paradox: if it’s zero-sum, why do hedge funds survive long-term?

This is the **key intellectual puzzle** Kaminski raises.

If:

- losers disappear
    
- winners retire
    
- capital doesn’t magically appear
    

Then how does the system persist?

👉 Answer: **not all directional profits come from other speculators’ losses**

## 4. Where _skilled_ directional traders actually make money

Kaminski lists several **non-trivial profit sources**. Let’s unpack each.

---

### (1) Risk-transfer services (this is crucial)

This connects directly to your earlier questions.

Producers and end users:

- are _not_ trying to maximise expected return
    
- they are willing to **pay to reduce variance**
    

Speculators:

- absorb that risk
    
- demand compensation
    

So profits come from:

- producers accepting lower prices
    
- consumers paying higher prices
    

This is **not zero-sum speculation**.  
It’s **insurance economics**.

Directional exposure arises as a _byproduct_ of providing this service.

---

### (2) Exploiting behavioural biases & market imperfections

Markets are not perfectly rational, especially in energy.

Examples:

- panic during weather events
    
- regulatory uncertainty
    
- overreaction to storage numbers
    
- herd behaviour around headlines
    

A trader who:

- understands _how people misreact_
    
- positions early
    
- exits before narratives collapse
    

Isn’t “betting direction” blindly — they’re exploiting **predictable human behaviour**.

---

### (3) Relative value trading (this is how real pros think)

Kaminski explicitly says:

> Skilled traders **do not usually bet on outright price direction**

Instead, they trade:

- gas vs oil
    
- power node A vs node B
    
- summer vs winter
    
- nearby vs deferred months
    

These are **structural relationships**, not guesses.

Bad example (his sarcasm):

> Betting gas vs oil on an MMBtu basis as if equilibrium must exist

Good examples:

- pipeline congestion vs hub prices
    
- refinery outages vs crack spreads
    
- storage constraints vs seasonal spreads
    

These trades are:

- research-heavy
    
- information-driven
    
- asymmetrical
    

---

### (4) Algorithmic / microstructure exploitation

Very short-lived inefficiencies:

- order book imbalance
    
- latency advantages
    
- exchange mechanics
    

These are:

- capital-intensive
    
- tech-heavy
    
- skill-based
    

Again: not simple directional bets.

---

### (5) Weather superiority = informational edge

Weather _is_ energy demand.

Firms with:

- better meteorologists
    
- better models
    
- faster interpretation
    

Are effectively:

> **front-running slower participants**

This is not illegal.  
It’s **competitive intelligence**.

---

### (6) Fundamental intelligence = slow front-running

This is subtle but powerful.

If you know:

- production outages
    
- pipeline maintenance
    
- LNG cargo movements
    
- regulatory bottlenecks
    

Before prices reflect them, you’re:

- taking directional exposure
    
- with informational asymmetry
    

This is how **merchant firms with physical assets dominate financial players**.

## 5. Why regulators tried to kill proprietary trading (Volcker Rule)

After 2008:

- banks were using deposit-backed balance sheets
    
- to take speculative bets
    

Volcker Rule:

- bans proprietary trading at banks
    
- forces desks to shut or spin off
    

But here’s the problem Kaminski highlights…

---

## 6. The impossible regulatory line-drawing problem

The Act allows:

- market making
    
- hedging
    
- customer trades
    

But:

- **a trade is a trade**
    
- same instruments
    
- same books
    
- same systems
    

How do you tell if a position is:

- inventory?
    
- hedge?
    
- speculative?
    

Answer: **you often can’t**, even internally.

Market making:

- requires inventory
    
- involves risk
    
- looks speculative
    

Hedging:

- can create residual risk
    
- can be intentionally incomplete
    

Customer trades:

- can embed views
    
- can be opportunistically sized
    

This ambiguity is structural, not accidental.

---

## 7. Likely outcome: migration, not elimination

Kaminski predicts (correctly, in hindsight):

- energy trading doesn’t disappear
    
- it moves to:
    
    - hedge funds
        
    - merchant energy companies
        
    - less regulated entities
        

Risk moves — it doesn’t vanish.

---

## 8. The deep takeaway (this is the part to internalise)

Directional trading is **not**:

- gambling
    
- coin flipping
    
- pure zero-sum warfare
    

At its best, it is:

- risk intermediation
    
- information exploitation
    
- structural arbitrage
    
- behavioural edge
    

At its worst, it is:

- ego
    
- leverage
    
- luck mistaken for skill
    

And the most dangerous cases are:

> when speculative bets are disguised as “hedging” and nobody wants to ask hard questions.

This ties directly back to:

- Dunning–Kruger
    
- organisational silos
    
- weak middle-office challenge
