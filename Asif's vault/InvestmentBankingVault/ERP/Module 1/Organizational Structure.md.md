## Purpose of this chapter

This chapter explains **how an energy trading business is organised**, _why that structure exists_, and _where profits and risks actually come from_. It also warns about **human failure modes** (ego, information asymmetry, Dunning–Kruger) that can destroy trading organisations if governance is weak.

---

## 1. Why energy trading organisations are hard to run

### Information asymmetry

Energy markets are **highly specialised, tightly coupled niches** (pipelines, power nodes, auctions, constraints). A trader can become _world‑class_ in a very narrow domain very quickly.

**Problem:**

- Knowledge becomes **concentrated in individuals**
    
- Managers cannot easily challenge specialists
    
- Success in a narrow niche creates _illusion of global competence_
    

### Dunning–Kruger effect (applied to trading floors)

Kaminski highlights a dangerous pattern:

- Incompetent but confident people appear decisive under pressure
    
- They rise during lucky streaks
    
- Eventually they cause catastrophic losses
    

This is why **process, separation of duties, and governance** matter more than individual brilliance.

---

## 2. What actually defines an “energy trading operation”

Energy trading is **not defined by owning assets**, but by **how risk is taken and managed**.

Key defining characteristics:

- Core assets are **contracts**, not physical infrastructure
    
- High portfolio turnover
    
- Profits measured using **mark‑to‑market (MTM)**
    
- Activities managed at the **book / portfolio** level
    
- Exploitation of:
    
    - arbitrage
        
    - structural inefficiencies
        
    - optionality
        
    - directional views (even if unspoken)
        

This definition includes:

- banks
    
- hedge funds
    
- utilities
    
- oil & gas majors
    
- merchant energy firms
    

**Important insight:** many firms _deny_ they are traders — but structurally, they are.

## 3. Sources of competitive advantage in energy trading

### Physical footprint advantage

Firms with **physical operations** (pipelines, plants, storage, shipping):

- see real‑time system stress
    
- get early signals
    
- understand constraints before prices move
    

This gives them an **information edge** over purely financial players.

### Balance sheet advantage

- lower regulatory overhead than banks
    
- ability to warehouse risk
    
- ability to structure long‑dated deals

## 4. Front–Middle–Back office model (why it exists)

This structure is copied from banks to **prevent self‑deception and fraud**.

|Function|Core purpose|
|---|---|
|Front Office|Take risk & execute trades|
|Middle Office|Measure & control risk|
|Back Office|Record, settle & report|

Boundaries are _intentionally enforced_ but _practically fuzzy_.

---

## 5. Front Office (where P&L is created)

### Traders

Execute **standardised, repeatable transactions**:

- futures
    
- swaps
    
- options
    
- basis trades
    

Motivations:

- speculation
    
- market making
    
- hedging
    
- client facilitation
    

Traders are typically organised by:

- commodity (gas, power, oil, coal)
    
- geography
    
- physical vs financial
    
- product type (NYMEX, basis, options)
    

### Originators (Marketing / Structuring)

Work on **long‑term, bespoke, high‑volume deals**.

Characteristics:

- months of negotiation
    
- heavy legal & regulatory risk
    
- complex embedded options
    
- requires quants, lawyers, risk, structuring
    

**Mental model difference:**

- Traders = hunters (opportunistic)
    
- Originators = farmers (relationship‑driven)
    

---

## 6. Trading vs Marketing (critical distinction)

|Trading|Marketing|
|---|---|
|Short‑term|Long‑term|
|Opportunistic|Relationship‑based|
|Can be financial|Always physical|
|Directional / hedging|Supply‑chain driven|

They are **mutually dependent**:

- Marketing creates flow & intelligence
    
- Trading provides hedging & balance sheet
    

**Conflict risk:**  
Traders can manipulate price curves around large origination deals to capture P&L.

**Mitigation:**

- align traders & originators under same management
    

---

## 7. Middle Office (risk is its mandate)

Middle office = **independent conscience of the firm**.

Key responsibilities:

- define trading policy
    
- enforce limits
    
- monitor breaches
    
- validate valuation models
    

### Trading policy includes:

- position limits
    
- sensitivity limits (Greeks)
    
- VaR limits
    
- escalation procedures
    

Middle office should **never report to front office**.

---

## 8. The quant organisation dilemma

Where should quants sit?

### Desk‑aligned quants

**Pros:**

- fast feedback
    
- market realism
    
- rapid model development
    

**Cons:**

- siloed knowledge
    
- inconsistent models
    
- risk of “going native” (becoming trader mouthpieces)
    

### Centralised quant group

**Pros:**

- consistency
    
- governance
    
- economies of scale
    

**Cons:**

- disconnected from markets
    
- overused & undervalued
    

### Kaminski’s preferred compromise

- semi‑permanent desk assignment
    
- strong central leadership
    

Still politically fragile.

---

## 9. Structuring & risk parallelism

Some firms create:

- **in‑business risk** (pre‑deal advisory)
    
- **independent risk** (approval & control)
    

Same trade‑offs as quant placement.

---

## 10. Back Office (where operational risk hides)

Responsibilities:

- price, vol, correlation curves
    
- daily P&L
    
- confirmations
    
- collateral management
    
- settlements
    
- physical scheduling
    

### System fragmentation problem

- multiple systems for physical vs financial
    
- poor integration
    
- heavy spreadsheet usage
    

**Key warning:**  
Spreadsheets are a **major source of corporate risk** due to:

- opaque logic
    
- lack of validation
    
- manual processes
    

---

## 11. Big takeaway

Energy trading failures are rarely caused by:

- lack of intelligence
    

They are caused by:

- information silos
    
- ego
    
- poor governance
    
- weak separation of duties
    
- misaligned incentives
